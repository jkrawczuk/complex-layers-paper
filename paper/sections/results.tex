\subsection*{Colon cancer benchmark}
The colon cancer benchmark did not show large accuracy gains from building many successive neurons.
Across both feature-count regimes, the best mean vote accuracy was reached early: at $n=4$ for \texttt{cpl},
at $n=3$ for \texttt{svm}, and already at $n=1$ for \texttt{logreg}.
Thus, in this real-data setting, neuron depth is better interpreted as a diagnostic of how each learner
distributes signal across successive sparse fits than as a mechanism that consistently improves accuracy.

The feature-count regime affected the learners differently.
For \texttt{cpl}, moving from $C_{\mathrm{low}}$ to $C_{\mathrm{high}}$ substantially improved the best mean vote
accuracy, from 0.7710 to 0.8493.
For \texttt{svm} and \texttt{logreg}, the two regimes were closer in predictive performance, although
\texttt{logreg} with $C_{\mathrm{high}}$ achieved the highest colon cancer accuracy in the table
(0.8667 at $n=1$).
This suggests that the broader first neuron was especially beneficial for the \texttt{cpl} formulation, whereas
the other two learners already captured most of their useful signal under the narrower setting.

The realized depth gives a second view of the same regularization effect.
The high-feature regimes selected more features in the first neuron, but they also built at least as many neurons
as their low-feature counterparts.
This is not a contradiction: under the objective convention used here, smaller $C$ imposes stronger effective L1
regularization, so later low-feature neurons can stop because no additional feature is worth selecting.
Larger $C$ weakens this effect and allows nonzero later neurons to be fitted even after broader earlier neurons.

The weighted vote based on training-fold balanced accuracy did not materially change these conclusions.
It either matched the best unweighted result or stayed very close to it, indicating that the main colon cancer
pattern is driven by the base sparse fits and the feature-count regime rather than by the voting rule itself.
The corresponding high-feature diagnostic trajectories are provided in the Appendix as a robustness check for the
patterns summarized in Table~\ref{tab:colon_summary}.

Figure~\ref{fig:colon_sparse_accuracy} shows why the later-neuron results should be interpreted cautiously.
For all three learners, the accuracy of the single $n$-th neuron decreases with depth, and the trajectories become
less stable near the end of the fitted sequence.
The weighted-vote curve is almost indistinguishable from the uniform-vote curve over most depths, so the figure
supports the table-level conclusion that training-based vote weights do not materially alter the colon cancer
results.
This instability coincides with a decreasing number of cross-validation splits in which that neuron depth is
available, so the right-hand ends of the trajectories summarize fewer fitted neurons than the early depths.
The selected-feature row shows a steady narrowing of later neurons for \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm}, with the
decline being more gradual for \texttt{svm}; for \texttt{logreg}, even the second and third neurons are not fitted
in all splits.
The optimization diagnostics are consistent with this picture.
Training loss increases with depth, indicating that later neurons are fitted on progressively weaker remaining
feature sets rather than on an easier residual problem.
The L1 norm generally decreases, but this should be read together with the selected-feature counts: later neurons
also use fewer features, so the norm trajectory is not an independent measure of model quality.

In the low-feature regime, later depths sometimes showed higher mean predictive performance, but these comparisons
must be read together with the available-split counts.
For \texttt{cpl} with $C_{\mathrm{low}}$, accuracy increased from 0.8180 at $n=1$ to 0.8430 at $n=3$, while
the first neuron selected 5.03 features on average.
For \texttt{svm} with $C_{\mathrm{low}}$, the best mean vote accuracy was 0.8500 at $n=3$, compared with 0.8250 at
$n=1$.
The first neuron selected 6.42 features on average.
For \texttt{logreg} with $C_{\mathrm{low}}$, the table reports a higher mean accuracy at $n=2$ than at $n=1$
(0.7424 versus 0.6950), but this is a coverage-conditioned effect rather than a direct change in the unweighted
binary vote.
The second neuron was available in 59 of the 100 splits; within those same 59 splits, the unweighted vote at
$n=2$ is identical to the first-neuron prediction because ties are resolved by the first neuron.
Thus, the higher mean at $n=2$ indicates that splits in which a second \texttt{logreg} neuron could be fitted were
also splits where the first neuron already performed better, while the weighted vote can still change at $n=2$
because the two neurons receive unequal training-derived weights.

In the high-feature regime, the first neuron often captured a larger fraction of the true signal, but additional
neurons contributed less useful information.
This was clearest for \texttt{logreg} with $C_{\mathrm{high}}$, where the first neuron already achieved the best mean
accuracy of 0.8590 and selected 27.58 features on average.
For \texttt{svm} with $C_{\mathrm{high}}$, the best mean accuracy was 0.8490 at $n=4$.
For \texttt{cpl} with $C_{\mathrm{high}}$, the best mean accuracy was 0.8480 at $n=3$.

Weighted voting again gave a more nuanced picture than a uniform improvement.
When only depths available in all 100 cross-validation splits were considered, the weighted vote reached the
same best accuracy as the unweighted vote for high-feature \texttt{logreg} (0.8590 at $n=1$), and a very similar
value for high-feature \texttt{svm} (0.8420 at $n=3$ versus an unweighted best of 0.8490 at $n=4$).
It did not improve the best accuracy of low-feature \texttt{cpl} or low-feature \texttt{svm}.
The clearest weighted-vote gain appeared for low-feature \texttt{logreg}, where the
weighted vote reached 0.7678 at $n=2$ compared with the unweighted value of 0.7424.

As for the colon cancer benchmark, the trajectory figures focus on the low-feature regime, where successive
neurons are most diagnostic of how predictive performance changes across depth. High-feature behavior is reported
in Table~\ref{tab:synthetic_performance}, in the text above, and in the Appendix diagnostic panel.

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic_sparse_accuracy} shows a clearer separation between the behavior of individual neurons
and the behavior of the cumulative vote.
For \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm}, the accuracy of the single $n$-th neuron drops after the first few depths,
whereas the uniform and weighted votes remain comparatively stable over the same range.
The two aggregate-vote curves are also very close to one another, indicating that the main effect comes from
aggregating successive neurons rather than from the particular training-based weighting scheme.
This indicates that later neurons are weak as standalone classifiers but can still preserve enough complementary
information to avoid degrading the aggregate vote immediately.
As in the colon cancer benchmark, the available-split row is essential for interpretation: the last depths are
based on fewer cross-validation splits, especially for \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{logreg}, and should not be read as
equally stable estimates.
The selected-feature trajectories also differ by learner: \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{logreg} become narrower with
depth, while \texttt{svm} first broadens and then contracts, suggesting a different path through the remaining
feature space.
The training diagnostics again show increasing loss and decreasing L1 norm across depth, consistent with later
neurons being fitted on progressively weaker residual feature sets rather than on easier classification problems.
Because the synthetic benchmark has known informative and noise features, the next subsection examines whether
these predictive trajectories correspond to recovery of the planted signal.

\subsection*{Built depth as a model property}
The nominal maximum depth was 31 neurons, but the realized depth depended strongly on the learner and
regularization.
On the colon cancer benchmark, the realized depth ranged from 3 neurons for low-feature \texttt{logreg} to 31 neurons
for high-feature \texttt{cpl} and high-feature \texttt{svm}.
On the synthetic benchmark, realized depth was markedly smaller, ranging from 3 neurons for low-feature
\texttt{logreg} to 13 neurons for high-feature \texttt{svm}.
This supports the view that neuron depth is not only a reporting axis but also an empirical signature of how
aggressively each base learner exhausts the remaining feature space.
