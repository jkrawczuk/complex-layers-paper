\subsection*{Colon cancer benchmark}
The colon cancer benchmark did not show large accuracy gains from building many successive neurons.
Across both feature-count regimes, the best mean vote accuracy was reached early: at $n=4$ for \texttt{cpl},
at $n=3$ for \texttt{svm}, and already at $n=1$ for \texttt{logreg}.
Thus, in this real-data setting, neuron depth is better interpreted as a diagnostic of how each learner
distributes signal across successive sparse fits than as a mechanism that consistently improves accuracy.

The feature-count regime affected the learners differently.
For \texttt{cpl}, moving from $C_{\mathrm{low}}$ to $C_{\mathrm{high}}$ substantially improved the best mean vote
accuracy, from 0.7710 to 0.8493.
For \texttt{svm} and \texttt{logreg}, the two regimes were closer in predictive performance, although
\texttt{logreg} with $C_{\mathrm{high}}$ achieved the highest colon cancer accuracy in the table
(0.8667 at $n=1$).
This suggests that the broader first neuron was especially beneficial for the \texttt{cpl} formulation, whereas
the other two learners already captured most of their useful signal under the narrower setting.

The realized depth gives a second view of the same regularization effect.
The high-feature regimes selected more features in the first neuron, but they also built at least as many neurons
as their low-feature counterparts.
This is not a contradiction: under the objective convention used here, smaller $C$ imposes stronger effective L1
regularization, so later low-feature neurons can stop because no additional feature is worth selecting.
Larger $C$ weakens this effect and allows nonzero later neurons to be fitted even after broader earlier neurons.

The weighted vote based on training-fold balanced accuracy did not materially change these conclusions.
It either matched the best unweighted result or stayed very close to it, indicating that the main colon cancer
pattern is driven by the base sparse fits and the feature-count regime rather than by the voting rule itself.
The corresponding high-feature diagnostic trajectories are provided in the Appendix as a robustness check for the
patterns summarized in Table~\ref{tab:colon_summary}.

Figure~\ref{fig:colon_sparse_accuracy} shows why the later-neuron results should be interpreted cautiously.
For all three learners, the accuracy of the single $n$-th neuron decreases with depth, and the trajectories become
less stable near the end of the fitted sequence.
The weighted-vote curve is almost indistinguishable from the uniform-vote curve over most depths, so the figure
supports the table-level conclusion that training-based vote weights do not materially alter the colon cancer
results.
This instability coincides with a decreasing number of cross-validation splits in which that neuron depth is
available, so the right-hand ends of the trajectories summarize fewer fitted neurons than the early depths.
The selected-feature row shows a steady narrowing of later neurons for \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm}, with the
decline being more gradual for \texttt{svm}; for \texttt{logreg}, even the second and third neurons are not fitted
in all splits.
The optimization diagnostics are consistent with this picture.
Training loss increases with depth, indicating that later neurons are fitted on progressively weaker remaining
feature sets rather than on an easier residual problem.
The L1 norm generally decreases, but this should be read together with the selected-feature counts: later neurons
also use fewer features, so the norm trajectory is not an independent measure of model quality.

The predictive results again show that useful information is concentrated near the beginning of the neuron
sequence.
In the low-feature regime, \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm} improved modestly over the first few depths, reaching their
best mean vote accuracies at $n=3$.
For \texttt{logreg} with $C_{\mathrm{low}}$, the best tabulated mean occurs at $n=2$, but this value must be read as
coverage-conditioned rather than as a direct two-neuron voting gain.
With binary unweighted voting and the tie rule used here, the $n=2$ vote is identical to the first-neuron
prediction within any split where the second neuron exists.
Thus, the higher mean at $n=2$ mainly shows that splits in which a second \texttt{logreg} neuron could be fitted
were also splits where the first neuron already performed better; the weighted vote can still differ at $n=2$
because the two neurons receive unequal training-derived weights.

The high-feature regime is even more concentrated at early depths.
High-feature \texttt{logreg} achieved its best mean accuracy with the first neuron alone, whereas high-feature
\texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm} showed only small gains at $n=3$ and $n=4$, respectively.
Across these settings, weighted voting did not change the qualitative picture: the weighted and unweighted
trajectories were generally close, and the main signal came from whether later neurons could still be fitted and
what they selected, not from the particular vote-weighting scheme.

As for the colon cancer benchmark, the trajectory figures focus on the low-feature regime, where successive
neurons are most diagnostic of how predictive performance changes across depth. High-feature behavior is reported
in Table~\ref{tab:synthetic_performance}, in the text above, and in the Appendix diagnostic panel.

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic_sparse_accuracy} explains why later depths must be interpreted cautiously.
For \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm}, the single-neuron accuracy drops after the first few depths, even though the
aggregate vote remains comparatively stable.
The available-split row is therefore essential: the deepest points are based on fewer cross-validation splits,
especially for \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{logreg}, and should not be read as equally stable estimates.
The selected-feature trajectories also differ by learner, with \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{logreg} narrowing with
depth while \texttt{svm} first broadens and then contracts.
The training diagnostics show the same pattern from the optimization side: loss increases and the L1 norm
decreases as later neurons are fitted on progressively weaker residual feature sets.
Because the synthetic benchmark has known informative and noise features, the next subsection examines whether
these predictive trajectories correspond to recovery of the planted signal.

These recovery summaries separate two modes of behavior.
The high-feature settings recover more of the 12 informative features by the best-accuracy depth, but they do so
with lower precision because many additional noise features are selected at the same time.
This trade-off is strongest for high-feature \texttt{svm}, where cumulative recall is high but cumulative precision
is low.
In contrast, the low-feature settings are more selective.
Low-feature \texttt{cpl} keeps a comparatively high cumulative precision while increasing recall across the first
few neurons, whereas low-feature \texttt{svm} reaches a similar recall with a larger loss of precision.
The \texttt{logreg} configurations are more concentrated: low-feature \texttt{logreg} remains very precise but
recovers fewer informative features, while high-feature \texttt{logreg} captures much of the informative set in
the first neuron.

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic_sparse_recovery} shows how these table summaries arise across neuron depth.
For low-feature \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm}, cumulative informative-feature recall increases over the first few
neurons, confirming that later neurons can recover signal not selected by the first neuron.
At the same time, the per-neuron fractions show a rapid shift: later neurons contain less informative signal and
more noise, especially for \texttt{svm}.
Low-feature \texttt{logreg} behaves differently, selecting very few noise features but also reaching a much smaller
depth.
Thus, the synthetic benchmark clarifies the main limitation of adding neurons: later neurons can add missing
signal, but only up to the point where the remaining feature pool becomes dominated by noise.

\subsection*{Realized neuron depth}
Although the nominal maximum depth was 31 neurons, the number of neurons that could actually be fitted varied
substantially across learners, regularization regimes, and datasets.
On the colon cancer benchmark, realized depth ranged from 3 neurons for low-feature \texttt{logreg} to the full
31-neuron limit for high-feature \texttt{cpl} and high-feature \texttt{svm}.
On the synthetic benchmark, the sequences were shorter overall, ranging from 3 neurons for low-feature
\texttt{logreg} to 13 neurons for high-feature \texttt{svm}.
Thus, neuron depth is not only an x-axis for reporting trajectories.
It is also an empirical outcome that reflects how quickly each learner exhausts the remaining feature space under
the imposed regularization.
