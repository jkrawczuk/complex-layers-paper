\section*{Discussion}

The final experiments support a narrower and more defensible interpretation of complex layers than a simple
claim that voting should monotonically improve classification.
Across both datasets, most useful predictive information was concentrated in the first few neurons.
Later neurons sometimes added complementary information, but the size and reliability of this effect depended on
the base learner, the feature-count regime, and the number of cross-validation splits in which the later neuron
could actually be fitted.

The real-data benchmark showed only small predictive gains from increasing depth.
On colon cancer, \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm} reached their best mean accuracies after several neurons,
whereas \texttt{logreg} was already best at the first neuron in both feature-count regimes.
This supports the view that sequential feature removal can expose additional predictive structure for some
learners, but it does not support a general expectation that deeper voting will improve accuracy.
The high-feature regime also showed that wider first neurons do not necessarily shorten the constructible
sequence: weaker effective regularization can still allow later nonzero neurons to be fitted in many splits.

The synthetic benchmark explains this behavior more directly because the informative features are known.
High-feature settings recovered more of the planted signal, but often with low feature precision because many
noise features were selected at the same time.
Low-feature settings were more selective.
In particular, low-feature \texttt{cpl} retained relatively high cumulative precision while increasing recall
over the first few neurons, whereas low-feature \texttt{svm} reached similar recall with a larger loss of
precision.
This separates two questions that are conflated by accuracy alone: whether later neurons improve prediction, and
whether they recover additional informative variables or mainly add noise.

The \texttt{logreg} results illustrate why coverage must be treated as part of the analysis rather than as a
minor plotting detail.
For low-feature \texttt{logreg} on the synthetic benchmark, the tabulated improvement at $n=2$ is conditioned on
the subset of splits in which a second neuron exists.
Within those splits, the unweighted two-neuron vote is identical to the first-neuron prediction under the
tie-breaking rule, so the higher mean does not represent a direct two-neuron voting effect.
It instead indicates that the splits supporting a second neuron were also easier for the first neuron.

Training-based weighted voting changed the conclusions little.
The weighted and uniform vote trajectories were usually close, indicating that the main effect came from the
successive sparse fits and the changing feature pool, not from the particular vote-weighting scheme.
This is useful practically: the complex-layer protocol can be interpreted primarily through neuron depth,
selected-feature behavior, and available-split counts, without relying on a delicate choice of voting weights.

A recurring outcome is the trade-off between signal recovery and feature purity.
Broader neurons often recovered more informative variables, but they also selected more noise.
Narrower neurons were cleaner, but they could require several layers before accumulating comparable signal and
could terminate earlier.
This supports reporting feature-recovery and depth diagnostics alongside accuracy, consistent with broader
feature-selection literature arguing that selected-feature behavior should be evaluated explicitly
\cite{saeys2007review,nogueira2018stability}.

Realized depth itself is therefore informative.
Some colon cancer configurations reached the nominal limit of 31 neurons, whereas the synthetic configurations
terminated after 3--13 neurons.
The number of constructible neurons is not a trivial implementation detail; it reflects how quickly each learner
exhausts useful separable structure under the imposed regularization.

Key limitations remain.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item The colon cancer benchmark is a small and classical microarray dataset, so its statistical variability is
high.
\item The synthetic benchmark is informative because it provides ground truth, but its conclusions remain
conditional on the chosen latent-source data-generation process.
\item Regularization values were calibrated to obtain comparable first-neuron widths, not to equalize the
effective penalty across models; direct numerical comparison of $C$ between learners is therefore not
meaningful.
\item The present study does not perform nested model selection, so the reported settings should be interpreted
as controlled analysis regimes rather than globally optimal hyperparameters.
\end{itemize}

Future work should extend this analysis to additional high-dimensional biological datasets, investigate the
stability of neuron-wise feature recovery across repeated resampling \cite{lukaszuk2024importance}, and study whether alternative stopping
rules can identify the depth at which complementary signal is exhausted and later neurons become primarily
noise-driven.
