\title{Complex layers of sparse formal neurons for high-dimensional classification}

\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.}
High-dimensional gene-expression datasets remain difficult classification benchmarks because they combine
very small sample sizes with thousands of correlated features. Sparse linear models can preserve
interpretability, but their predictive behavior depends on how sparsity is imposed and how successive feature
subsets are constructed. This study examines successive sparse formal neurons in a complex-layer construction,
focusing on how signal, sparsity, and noise evolve across neuron depth.

\textbf{Methods.}
We study two complementary benchmarks: a classical colon cancer microarray dataset with 62 samples, 2,000
features, and two diagnostic classes, and a synthetic binary dataset with known informative and noise
features. Three linear learners are evaluated within the same complex-layer protocol: \texttt{cpl}
(convex piecewise-linear; hinge loss with L1 regularization), \texttt{svm} (squared hinge loss with L1
regularization), and \texttt{logreg} (logistic loss with L1 regularization). In each layer, one sparse neuron is
trained and its selected features are removed before fitting the next neuron. Performance is assessed using
repeated stratified cross-validation. We summarize predictive accuracy, selected-feature counts, weighted voting
and, for the synthetic benchmark, feature recovery across neuron depth.

\textbf{Results.}
On colon cancer, additional neurons produced small and learner-dependent accuracy changes rather than a
monotonic improvement. The best mean vote accuracy was reached early: at $n=4$ for \texttt{cpl}, at $n=3$ for
\texttt{svm}, and already at $n=1$ for \texttt{logreg}. The feature-count regime mattered most for
\texttt{cpl}, whose best accuracy increased from 0.7710 in the low-feature regime to 0.8493 in the high-feature
regime; high-feature \texttt{logreg} achieved the highest colon cancer accuracy, 0.8667, with the first neuron
alone. Training-quality weighted voting was usually almost indistinguishable from uniform voting, so the main
patterns were driven by the sparse fits and feature-count regimes rather than by the voting rule. On the
synthetic benchmark, low-feature \texttt{cpl} and \texttt{svm} improved over the first few depths, whereas the
apparent low-feature \texttt{logreg} improvement at $n=2$ reflected the subset of splits in which a second neuron
could be built. Ground-truth-aware metrics showed a trade-off: broader first neurons recovered more informative
features at once but with lower precision, whereas narrower configurations recovered informative structure more
gradually across successive neurons.

\textbf{Conclusions.}
The complex-layer protocol is most useful here as a diagnostic framework rather than as a guarantee of improved
voting accuracy. Neuron depth reveals how each sparse learner distributes predictive signal, exhausts the
remaining feature pool, and trades informative-feature recall against selection precision. Later-depth summaries
must be read together with available-split counts, and training-based vote weighting should not be expected to
change the qualitative behavior when uniform and weighted trajectories are already close.
\end{abstract}
